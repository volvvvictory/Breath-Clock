# Breath Clock

**Built by Viktoria Volokitina**
Time Machines: Clocks in History of Technology and Contemporary Art
Professor Natalia Fedorova · Spring 2026

---

The atmosphere, Emanuele Coccia writes, is the oldest public share. What you exhale, the other inhales. Together with all animals and plants, we breathe in the same literal compound of carbon dioxide, nitrogen and oxygen, and the same metaphoric compound of present, past, and future. To inhale is to allow the world to enter. To exhale is to project yourself into the world.

Linear time we have nowadays is a construction. Jacques Le Goff showed how the mechanical clocks were imposed to extract ownership over time. Michel Foucault extended this idea: disciplinary institutions linearized time to transform living bodies into measurable units. The clock was always already a claim about what time is for. Before the mechanical clock, Camilla Power argues, communities organized collective life around the lunar cycle. For at least 30,000 years this first shared calendar remained visible to everyone.

Breath Clock returns control over time to the body. This visualization renders time through the collective breathing of the UAE's 10 million inhabitants. Average 16.854 breaths per minute drives the counter, the orrery animation, and the circadian waveform. Temperature data from Abu Dhabi adjusts the rhythm in real time, because the body responds to climate.

At the center, an epicycloid traces the mathematical relationship between breath and lunar cycle; the number of breaths fit inside one moon orbit determines the flower-like pattern's shape. Henri Bergson called lived time durée — duration as felt from inside, irreducible to measurement. The orrery draws that duration into a pattern.

The breath is the oldest commons, the first medium of collective life, predating any other method of time measurement.
